0. Scope, method, and how to read the evidence tags

0.1 What this document is

Three independent reverse-engineering studies, a comparison of the resulting architectures, and the design and implementation of a quarantine system that can hold these threats as evidence without ever letting them run.

The three families are not variants of one another and are not treated as such:

Class Year disclosed Actor Lives in
LoJax Firmware implant (UEFI rootkit) 2018 Sednit / APT28 SPI flash, BIOS region
MoonBounce Firmware implant (UEFI rootkit) 2022 APT41 (vendor assessment) SPI flash, CORE_DXE
BlackLotus UEFI bootkit 2023 Commodity, sold on forums EFI System Partition + NVRAM

The single most important distinction in this whole document, and the one most often blurred in secondary reporting: BlackLotus is not a firmware implant. It never writes to flash. LoJax and MoonBounce do. That difference determines whether replacing the disk helps, whether a firmware update helps, and whether the machine can be trusted again without physical intervention.

0.2 Evidence grading

Every non-trivial claim carries one of these tags. This is the discipline that keeps vendor summaries from being mistaken for reverse-engineering evidence.

Tag Meaning
[CONFIRMED] Directly supported by technical evidence: decompilation, byte-level structure, a named artifact in a published analysis.
[CORROBORATED] Supported by two or more independent sources that agree.
[INFERRED] A reasonable technical conclusion drawn from confirmed behaviour, but not itself directly observed.
[HYPOTHESIS] Plausible and consistent with evidence, but unproven. Stated so it can be attacked.
[UNKNOWN] Insufficient public evidence. Explicitly not guessed.

A claim that a vendor's marketing summary makes and that no technical publication supports stays at [HYPOTHESIS] no matter how often it is repeated. Where I could not obtain a sample, the text distinguishes documented behaviour from independently verified behaviour, and most of this study is the former: no live samples were executed at any point.

1. Executive technical summary

1. The three threats occupy different layers, and the layer decides the remediation. LoJax and MoonBounce live in SPI flash and survive OS reinstall, disk replacement and (for partial updates) firmware update. BlackLotus lives on the EFI System Partition and in a UEFI variable; reformatting the OS partition and the ESP removes it, which is why Microsoft's recovery guidance is a reformat rather than a reflash.

2. Two of the three did not exploit anything. LoJax and MoonBounce both required the attacker to already hold kernel-level access and then abused legitimate firmware-write interfaces. LoJax carried a signed, legitimate driver (RWEverything's RwDrv) to reach the flash. Neither needed a firmware vulnerability; they needed a misconfigured platform - BIOS write protections left off. BlackLotus is the inverse: it is not privileged at the firmware layer at all, and instead exploits a software trust failure (CVE-2022-21894, "baton drop") to defeat Secure Boot, then uses the Linux shim's MOK mechanism exactly as designed to make its unsigned bootkit load forever after.

3. Persistence quality is not the same as stealth. LoJax leaves a structural fingerprint: a new FFS file named SecDxe, a deleted AMI NTFS driver, and a changed free-space layout. MoonBounce leaves none - it inline-hooks an existing, legitimate CORE_DXE so the firmware volume list is byte-identical in structure and differs only in content. §3.8 reconstructs that mechanism end to end: five bytes of three boot-service prologues replaced by a call, a retfq tail patched in winload.efi to jump to shellcode at physical 0x98000, and an ExAllocatePool hook placed in ntoskrnl's relocation directory after forcing every kernel section to write-and-execute. BlackLotus leaves a great deal: files on the ESP, a MokList entry, and Measured Boot deltas. Detection difficulty therefore runs MoonBounce > LoJax > BlackLotus, while deployment ease runs the opposite way.

4. Secure Boot is not a firmware integrity control. This is the most valuable correction to make. Secure Boot governs what the firmware is willing to load from disk: boot managers, option ROMs, loaders. It does not, by itself, attest that the firmware in the SPI flash is the firmware the vendor shipped. LoJax added an unsigned DXE driver to firmware and ESET's own recommendation to enable Secure Boot [CONFIRMED, ESET §6] should be read as one layer, not the mechanism that stops a firmware implant. The mechanisms that actually constrain flash writes are the platform's own: BIOSWE/BLE/SMM_BWP, the descriptor's region permissions, protected ranges (PR0-PR5), FLOCKDN, and Boot Guard.

5. Eradication is a hardware procedure, not a software one. For LoJax and MoonBounce: reflash the SPI flash from a trusted image with a hardware programmer, or replace the board; a vendor firmware update removes it only if the update rewrites the entire BIOS region, which is not guaranteed [CONFIRMED, ESET §6]. For BlackLotus: reformat OS + ESP, revoke the attacker's MOK, apply dbx revocations, and remove the third-party UEFI CA where it is not needed [CONFIRMED, Microsoft; ESET].

6. Encryption is containment, not eradication, and it is weaker than it looks. A vault that encrypts at ingestion prevents casual execution: backups, AV crawlers, file previewers, an analyst double-clicking the wrong file. It does not prevent execution that happens before encryption, and it does not reach code already resident in firmware, which runs at boot regardless of what is on disk. The design in §9 is therefore layered so that no single mechanism is load-bearing: per-object keys, split-knowledge KEK, dual control, enclave-only decryption, hash-chained custody, and an ingestion appliance that has no writable path for plaintext.

7. The defensible conclusion. A quarantine system can guarantee that a preserved sample is unmodified and unexecuted between ingestion and retrieval. It cannot guarantee that the machine that produced the sample was clean, nor that the analysis host is clean. Those remain human and physical controls, and a design that claims otherwise is selling something.

2. LoJax: the first in-the-wild UEFI rootkit

Primary source: ESET Research, "LoJax: First UEFI rootkit found in the wild, courtesy of the Sednit group", September 2018, 24 pp.

2.1 What it is

LoJax is the name ESET gave to two things that arrived together: a trojanized build of an old (2008-era) Absolute Software LoJack/Computrace small agent, and a malicious UEFI DXE driver (SecDxe) whose only job is to keep dropping that agent onto the machine. The pairing matters: the agent is ordinary Windows crimeware-grade code, and the driver is what makes it survive everything.

Attribution to Sednit/APT28 is based on co-located Sednit tooling and C2 infrastructure overlap, stated at "high confidence" by ESET [CONFIRMED as ESET's assessment]. Victims were government entities in the Balkans and Central/Eastern Europe, in small numbers [CONFIRMED].

The strategic insight Sednit had is the one defenders still under-rate: the LoJack anti-theft agent is designed to survive disk wipes and reinstalls, because that is what anti-theft software must do. Its architecture was already a rootkit. Sednit did not have to invent firmware persistence; they had to steal an existing, signed, OEM-blessed persistence channel.

2.2 Why the LoJack architecture was abusable

Computrace's circa-2008 design, as documented by ESET:

  1. A UEFI/BIOS module runs at boot.
  2. It finds a FAT/FAT32/NTFS partition, backs up autochk.exe, and overwrites it with a dropper.
  3. autochk.exe runs early in Windows initialisation (BootExecute), drops rpcnetp.exe and registers it as a service, then restores the original.
  4. rpcnetp.exe is a tiny agent that converts itself to a DLL in memory, injects into svchost.exe and iexplore.exe, and talks to Absolute's server.
  5. That server can instruct it to download and install the full recovery agent.

Two design flaws made this a gift to an attacker [CONFIRMED]:

  • No authentication on the C2 channel. ESET quotes prior research: if an adversary controls the server the small agent talks to, they can make it download and run arbitrary code.
  • The C2 address is in a config file obfuscated with a single-byte XOR (key 0xB5, identical across agents), with no integrity validation. Anyone with write access to %WINDIR% can repoint the agent.

That is the whole "initial infection" story for the userland half: no exploit, no memory corruption, just a writable configuration file and a trusting client.

2.3 The toolchain: how they reached the flash

ESET recovered the actual operator tooling, which is what makes this report unusually well-evidenced. Three named binaries [CONFIRMED]:

Tool Role ESET detection
info_efi.exe Enumerate/dump firmware, wraps a kernel driver Win32/Agent.ZXZ
ReWriter_read.exe Dump the SPI flash to a file Win32/SPIFlash.A
ReWriter_binary.exe Patch the dumped image and write it back Win32/SPIFlash.A

Both flash tools ride on RWEverything's RwDrv, a legitimately signed kernel driver shipped with a hardware-inspection utility. This is a textbook BYOVD (bring your own vulnerable driver) move, in 2018, for firmware access.

The read path [CONFIRMED]: locate the Root Complex Register Block base by reading PCI configuration offset 0xF0 via RwDrv IOCTL 0x22840; then drive the chipset's SPI controller registers (HSFS/HSFC/FDATAX) through the canonical sequence - set cycle type to read, program the flash address, set the byte count, set FGO, poll SCIP, read data - looping over the region. This is the same primitive CHIPSEC exposes today, which is why CHIPSEC is the right detection and acquisition tool.

Then the tool validates its own dump by parsing the flash descriptor and summing the BIOS, GbE and ME region sizes, comparing the total against the BIOS Flash Primary region register. A careful, engineering-minded implementation: this is not script-kiddie firmware work [CONFIRMED].

The patch path [CONFIRMED]: parse FFS volumes, looking for four anchors -

  • Ip4Dxe {8f92960f-2880-4659-b857-915a8901bdc8} - used only as a marker that this volume holds DXE drivers;
  • DXE Core - a second candidate volume;
  • NtfsDxe {768bedfd-7b4b-4c9f-b2ff-6377e3387243} - the AMI NTFS DXE driver, which gets deleted;
  • SmiFlash {bc327dbd-b982-4f55-9f79-056ad7e987c5} - noted, stored, never used.

It then constructs an EFI_FFS_FILE_HEADER, embeds a compressed PE image plus a UI section naming the file SecDxe, and writes it into the free space at the end of the chosen volume. It removes the AMI NTFS driver by overwriting it with 0xFF and sliding the trailing volume contents down - because the AMI driver is read-only, and the attacker needs a write-capable NTFS driver.

The SmiFlash finding deserves a note. ESET writes that this image is vulnerable and that "we believe that Sednit's operators might be working on some exploit for these vulnerabilities" [ESET's own wording: HYPOTHESIS]. In its observed state, the tool only works on systems whose BIOS region write protections are misconfigured or absent, on hardware older than the PCH generation introduced around 2008 [CONFIRMED].

2.4 The write-protection story, which is the real lesson

ESET's explanation of why this worked is the most reusable part of the paper:

  • BIOSWE (BIOS Write Enable) in BIOS_CNTL must be 1 to write the BIOS region at all.
  • BLE (BIOS Lock Enable) is supposed to protect it: setting BIOSWE raises an SMI, and the SMI handler clears it. But the handler is firmware's responsibility - if the vendor never wrote it, BLE is decorative. And there is a race condition (Kallenberg & Wojtczuk, "Speed Racer") that bypasses it entirely: one thread continuously sets BIOSWE while another writes flash.
  • SMM_BWP (SMM BIOS Write Protect Disable), introduced with the PCH, fixes the race by requiring all cores to be in SMM for the BIOS region to be writable - but again, only if firmware sets it.

So the security of the SPI flash was, and largely still is, a firmware configuration property, not a platform guarantee. ReWriter_binary reads BIOS_CNTL and branches on what it finds: it takes the easy path when BIOSWE is already set, and otherwise attempts to exploit the configuration it meets. It does not check protected ranges (PRx) at all, which is exactly why PRx is the control that actually stops this class [INFERRED from ESET's note that "other existing mechanisms, like BIOS Range Write Protection, are left aside since they are not checked by ReWriter_binary"].

2.5 The rootkit itself: SecDxe

SecDxe is a DXE driver. The DXE Dispatcher loads it during the Driver Execution Environment phase, before the OS loader exists, which is what makes it early enough to matter [CONFIRMED].

It embeds a whole NTFS driver. ESET's evidence that Sednit did not write it is strong: strings reference c:\edk2\NtfsPkg\NtfsDxe\ntfs\*.c - mft.c, inode.c, volume.c, logfile.c, lcnalloc.c, security.c and more - which is the directory layout of Hacking Team's leaked vector-edk project, a thin wrapper around ntfs-3g. Function-by-function comparison, including the same LockedByMe lock variable, matches the leaked source [CONFIRMED].

What it does at boot [CONFIRMED]:

UEFI DXE phase
  └─ SecDxe (malicious, in BIOS region of SPI flash)
       ├─ loads embedded NTFS DXE driver (Hacking Team / ntfs-3g)
       ├─ mounts the Windows NTFS volume
       ├─ writes  %WINDIR%\SysWOW64\rpcnetp.exe      (System32 on 32-bit)
       ├─ writes  %WINDIR%\SysWOW64\autoche.exe
       └─ patches the SYSTEM registry hive FILE on disk:
            HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Control\Session Manager\BootExecute
            "autocheck autochk *"   ->   "autocheck autoche *"
                 (single byte: the 'k' of autochk becomes 'e')

Windows early boot
  └─ smss.exe executes BootExecute -> autoche.exe
       ├─ creates a service for rpcnetp.exe (reusing the legitimate Computrace service name)
       └─ restores BootExecute to "autocheck autochk *"

Windows running
  └─ rpcnetp.exe (service, trojanized 2008 Computrace agent)
       ├─ self-converts to DLL in memory, injects into svchost.exe and iexplore.exe
       └─ contacts attacker-controlled C2, can download/execute further modules

Note the elegance of the registry edit: it is a one-byte file-level patch of a hive file, performed from firmware, at a point where no OS security product exists to see it. autoche.exe then cleans up after itself, so the registry is correct again by the time anyone logs in [CONFIRMED].

ESET never observed the agent actually downloading additional modules, but states the functionality exists [CONFIRMED as an observation of absence; the capability is INFERRED from the inherited Computrace code path].

2.7 Why LoJax was detectable

Paradoxically, the crudest implant is the easiest to spot structurally. Its fingerprint is a firmware volume that does not match the vendor's image:

  • an FFS file named SecDxe with a UI section, in a volume that should not contain it;
  • a missing NtfsDxe (768bedfd-...) where the OEM shipped one;
  • free space at the end of a volume that no longer matches the vendor build;
  • an embedded NTFS DXE driver inside a file that has no business carrying one;
  • strings c:\edk2\NtfsPkg\NtfsDxe\... that exist in no shipping OEM firmware;
  • firmware volumes whose total size or checksums disagree with the vendor release for that exact model and version.

That is a diff against a known-good image, which is tractable at fleet scale if you have vendor images per SKU. §7 turns this into a procedure.

3. MoonBounce: the implant that changed nothing and everything

Primary source: Kaspersky GReAT (Lechtik, Berdnikov, Legezo, Borisov), "MoonBounce: the dark side of UEFI firmware", 20 January 2022.

3.1 What it is, and why it is the real technical milestone

MoonBounce is a modification of an existing, legitimate firmware component - CORE_DXE, the DXE Foundation - rather than the addition of a new module. That single design choice is the most important advance in this whole family tree, and it is why the detection section of this report looks the way it does.

LoJax added a file. A file can be enumerated. MoonBounce added nothing: it took a module the platform already ships, patched its function prologues with inline hooks, and appended shellcode to the end of the same PE image. Enumerate the firmware volumes and you see exactly what the OEM shipped, with the same file count, same GUIDs, same names, same sizes-on-disk. You only see it if you compare bytes against a known-good image, or if something at runtime notices that AllocatePool does not start with the instructions it should [CONFIRMED, Kaspersky].

3.2 Discovery path

Detected through Kaspersky's Firmware Scanner, integrated into their products since 2019, acting on logs from an inspected machine; the implant itself was noticed in spring 2021 [CONFIRMED]. This is itself a finding: the victim did not detect it. A security product that logs firmware-component hashes and diffs them against a vendor baseline did. That is the operational model §7 recommends.

3.3 Architecture

CORE_DXE is loaded early in the DXE phase and is responsible for initialising core data structures - among them the EFI_BOOT_SERVICES table, a struct of function pointers to routines inside CORE_DXE itself [CONFIRMED].

MoonBounce hooks three of those pointers, redirecting them into shellcode appended to the CORE_DXE image [CONFIRMED]:

Hooked boot service Why that one
AllocatePool Position and control memory allocations, so attacker-controlled buffers land where the next stage will look for them
CreateEventEx Gain a callback at chosen boot events rather than polling
ExitBootServices The exact handoff point from firmware to the OS loader - the last moment firmware code can touch the machine before Windows owns it

From there the chain continues in the loader and the kernel:

SPI flash (BIOS region)
  └─ CORE_DXE  ── inline hooks in AllocatePool / CreateEventEx / ExitBootServices
                  + appended shellcode
        │
        ├─ at ExitBootServices: hook functions inside the Windows loader
        │
        ├─ place a malicious driver into the kernel's address space
        │     (runs in early kernel init, before most security software)
        │
        ├─ driver injects the user-mode stage into svchost.exe
        │     (legitimate process, expected network behaviour)
        │
        └─ user-mode stage fetches  hxxp://mb.glbaitech[.]com/mboard.dll
                                    and runs it in memory

[CONFIRMED, Kaspersky public report. An earlier revision of this document left the loader and kernel hook targets as UNKNOWN because they are not in the public blog. They are resolved at function level in §3.8, from Kaspersky's separate technical-details document, which is now cited in §14 as a primary source.]

3.4 The parts that matter for defenders

Nothing touches the disk. The entire chain - shellcode, driver, user-mode stage, downloaded payload - is memory-resident. There is no dropped file, no service, no registry value, no scheduled task [CONFIRMED]. Every conventional DFIR artifact is absent by construction. If you image the disk of a MoonBounce victim and find nothing, you have learned nothing.

The payload is fetched, not embedded. The implant is a loader: it exists to get a small user-mode stage running in a trusted process with a network path. That keeps the flash modification small - important, because space in a firmware volume is limited and large appended blobs are easier to spot.

The infection vector is unknown. Kaspersky states plainly that they lack sufficient evidence to retrace how the firmware was infected, and assume it was remote [UNKNOWN, explicitly stated by the source]. Given that producing this implant requires reading the original image, parsing it, patching it and writing it back, the attacker needed kernel-level code execution plus a working SPI write path, and (unless they used an exploit) the same permissive BIOSWE/BLE/SMM_BWP/PRx configuration that LoJax needed [INFERRED].

Same-network context is unusually rich. Kaspersky also found, on the same network, the in-memory implant ScrambleCross/SideWalk, loaded by a C++ DLL called StealthVector which decrypts its shellcode with a modified ChaCha20. One StealthVector variant arrived via a patched wbemcomn.dll (MD5 c3b153347aed27435a18e789d8b67e0a) whose import address table had been extended to load wmiwk.dll whenever the WMI service started [CONFIRMED]. Sample loader wbwkem.dll (MD5 4d5eb9f6f501b4f6edf981a3c6c4d6fa) fetched compwm.bin from dev.kinopoisksu[.]com. This matters for triage: a MoonBounce case is not one artifact, it is a network-wide intrusion where the firmware implant is the persistence of last resort.

3.6 Detection difficulty: the honest assessment

MoonBounce is the hardest of the three to detect, and the reason is structural, not obfuscation:

  1. No added or removed file. FFS enumeration produces a clean inventory.
  2. No size change visible to a naive scanner. Shellcode is appended inside the existing PE's padding/slack; the FFS file size may be unchanged.
  3. Hooks are small. A 5-byte relative JMP at a function prologue, or a pointer swap in the boot services table, is invisible to string scanning and to signature databases that key on known-bad bytes.
  4. Nothing on disk, ever. Host forensics has nothing to find.
  5. It runs before every defensive control you own. Any EDR that depends on the OS being intact is downstream of the compromise.

The only robust detection is known-good comparison: hash every firmware volume, module and section at acquisition, and compare against the OEM's signed release for that exact model and firmware version. That requires an asset inventory that records firmware versions per machine - which most organisations do not have, and which is the actual gap to close.

3.7 Indicators

The CORE_DXE image differs from the vendor release. Concretely, look for:

  • a CORE_DXE PE whose section data extends beyond the vendor image's section layout, or whose padding contains non-0xFF/non-zero bytes;
  • function prologues in CORE_DXE that begin with a jump instead of the vendor's expected instructions (a byte-level diff finds this in seconds; nothing else does);
  • an EFI_BOOT_SERVICES table whose entries point outside the CORE_DXE image range at runtime;
  • network: mb.glbaitech[.]com, /mboard.dll;
  • memory: a driver in kernel space with no corresponding file on disk, injected into svchost.exe.

No file hashes are published for the MoonBounce flash modification itself, because it is a patch to a vendor-specific image and therefore differs per platform [CONFIRMED as an absence: Kaspersky publishes infrastructure IOCs and a Malpedia rule set, not a universal module hash].

The payload is appended to an unnamed section that follows .reloc, and contains both the shellcode and the malicious kernel driver. That single sentence explains why file-level inventory fails: a UI section can be renamed, but an unnamed section appended to a legitimate module does not change the file's GUID, name, or its place in the volume.

The hook discipline. All three boot-service hooks are installed identically: the first 5 bytes of the function prologue are replaced with a call to one shared hook routine. That routine identifies which function was called by examining the bytes immediately after the call, matches them against known patterns, and dispatches to the relevant handler [CONFIRMED]. This is compact, position-independent, and requires no import table, which is exactly what you want inside a firmware module.

Each handler follows the same three-step shape: restore the original prologue, save rcx, rdx, r8, r9, rsi, rdi (argument registers), do one piece of malicious work by calling the now-unhooked function, then restore registers and re-enter the original function with the caller's original arguments. The transition into Windows. The ExitBootServices handler takes its own return address from the stack and scans forward 0x158878 bytes for the byte pattern 41 55 48 CB (push r13; retfq) - the tail of OslArchTransferToKernel in winload.efi (or osloader.exe). It then:

  1. copies 0x229 bytes of shellcode to physical address 0x98000;
  2. overwrites the 48 CB (retfq) at the end of OslArchTransferToKernel with E9 <rel32> - a jump to 0x98000;
  3. restores registers and re-enters the unhooked ExitBootServices.

The hook depends on a specific structural feature of one Windows loader build (the retfq tail), which is why it is version-sensitive and why Kaspersky found it on a specific victim rather than universally [INFERRED from the fixed offsets].

Escalation inside the kernel. The OslArchTransferToKernel hook resolves exports from ntoskrnl.exe with a custom name-hash rather than by string:

        name_hash += index

# hash #2, used in the driver-mapping shellcode (ROR13 variant)
def fn_name_hash_ror13(f_name):
    f_hash = 0

Resolved in stage one [CONFIRMED]: 0x42790710 → ExRegisterCallback, 0x2802057D → ExAllocatePool, 0x1C88047D → MmMapIoSpace.

It then makes ntoskrnl.exe do something it should never do: for every section header in the mapped image it clears IMAGE_SCN_MEM_DISCARDABLE and sets IMAGE_SCN_MEM_EXECUTE | IMAGE_SCN_MEM_WRITE | IMAGE_SCN_MEM_NOT_PAGED. That is a system-wide W+X condition, created at boot, before any integrity policy can object. It copies 0xCC bytes of shellcode into the virtual address of ntoskrnl's relocation directory - memory that is executable, never pageable, and not covered by a code-integrity hash - and installs an E8 <rel32> inline hook at the start of ExAllocatePool.

When the kernel first calls ExAllocatePool, the hook (guarded by a one-shot global flag) calls MmMapIoSpace to map the shellcode allocated back in stage one, and jumps to it with three arguments: a pointer to the saved ExAllocatePool prologue, the ntoskrnl base, and the address of ExAllocatePool.

Loading the driver without a loader. The mapped shellcode performs the last firmware-to-kernel transition:

  • compares the saved prologue against the immediate 0x6F4EB841; if it matches, it clears the WP bit in CR0, restores the original ExAllocatePool bytes (unhooking), and restores CR0;
  • resolves ExAllocatePool, RtlInitAnsiString, RtlAnsiStringToUnicodeString and MmGetSystemRoutineAddress by ROR13 hash (0x0311B83F, 0x41EBE619, 0x1C4F5B64, 0x0ADC68C7);
  • manually maps the embedded driver PE: allocate, copy headers and sections, apply relocations, resolve imports through RtlInitAnsiString → RtlAnsiStringToUnicodeString → MmGetSystemRoutineAddress, free the string with RtlFreeUnicodeString;
  • jumps to the driver's entry point.

Note the deliberate un-hooking before doing the heavy work: once the driver is mapped, ExAllocatePool is pristine again. The implant spends as little time as possible in a modified state.

4. BlackLotus: the bootkit that beat Secure Boot honestly

Primary sources: ESET (Martin Smolár), "BlackLotus UEFI bootkit: Myth confirmed", 1 March 2023; Microsoft Defender Experts, "Guidance for investigating attacks using CVE-2022-21894", 11 April 2023; NSA, "BlackLotus Mitigation Guide", June 2023; Wack0's CVE-2022-21894 PoC and write-up.

4.1 What it is

A ~80 kB commercial UEFI bootkit, sold on criminal forums for around $5,000 since at least 6 October 2022, and the first publicly documented bootkit to run on a fully patched Windows 11 system with Secure Boot enabled [CONFIRMED, ESET; CORROBORATED, Microsoft].

It is not a firmware implant. It writes nothing to SPI flash. Its persistence is entirely on the EFI System Partition and in a UEFI NVRAM variable. This distinction is load-bearing for every remediation decision that follows.

4.2 The insight: don't break Secure Boot, borrow its trust

Secure Boot will not load an unsigned bootloader. BlackLotus therefore does two things in sequence:

  1. Temporarily break Secure Boot using CVE-2022-21894, so it can execute unsigned code once, before ExitBootServices.
  2. Use that one moment of authority to enroll its own Machine Owner Key in MokList, and swap in a legitimate Microsoft-signed shim as the boot loader.

After step 2, it no longer needs the vulnerability. shim will verify and launch grubx64.efi because the attacker's key is in the MOK database - which is precisely what MOK is for. The persistence mechanism is a documented, supported, signed feature working exactly as designed. Nothing is broken on subsequent boots. That is why revocation alone does not clean an infected machine, and why the recovery procedure is a reformat.

4.3 Installation

The installer comes in offline and online variants; the online variant downloads its vulnerable victims directly from the Microsoft symbol store [CONFIRMED, ESET]. The store is addressed by build-specific identifiers, so the form is the checkable part:

https://msdl.microsoft.com/download/symbols/<file>/<TimeDateStamp><SizeOfImage>/<file>

Three concrete URLs stood here until they were checked on 2 November 2026 and returned 404. They are not reproduced: the identifier pairs could not be verified against the symbol store, and an unverifiable identifier presented as evidence is worse than the pattern it illustrates. What the source supports is the channel and the mechanism, which is what the paragraph below relies on. tools/linkcheck.py is what caught this.

Note what this means: the attacker's payload is fetched from Microsoft, over TLS, with a valid signature. Any control that trusts "signed by Microsoft" is satisfied. Revocation (dbx) is the only answer, and revocation had not happened.

Preconditions [CONFIRMED]: local administrator, or SYSTEM. If not already elevated, the installer escalates through a UAC bypass (Program Compatibility Assistant). It reads the SecureBoot UEFI variable to learn whether it needs the exploit at all, and reads KUSER_SHARED_DATA (NtMajorVersion/NtMinorVersion) directly from memory to fingerprint the OS build.

Defanging the platform first [CONFIRMED]:

  • HVCI off - sets HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Control\DeviceGuard\Scenarios\HypervisorEnforcedCodeIntegrity\Enabled = 0 (only if the key exists). Without this, its unsigned kernel driver cannot load.
  • BitLocker off - enumerates Win32_EncryptableVolume via WMI (Root\CIMV2\Security\MicrosoftVolumeEncryption) and calls DisableKeyProtectors. Rationale, explicit in ESET's analysis: with TPM-bound BitLocker left on, the changed boot chain triggers a recovery prompt at next boot and tips off the victim. The attacker disables the tripwire before tripping it.
  • Original boot manager renamed - bootmgfw.efi becomes winload.efi in the ESP, kept as both a launch path and an uninstall path.

4.4 CVE-2022-21894 ("baton drop") in one paragraph

Windows Boot Applications (bootmgr.efi, hvloader.efi, winload.efi) allow a serialized Secure Boot policy to be removed from memory before it is consumed, by using the truncatememory BCD option [CONFIRMED, PoC write-up + ESET]. The attacker first forces the policy to be allocated above a known physical address using avoidlowmemory = 0x10000000, chain-loads a second BCD via the bcdfilepath element (custom:22000023) to get around the Windows 10+ ban on avoidlowmemory when VBS is on, and then sets truncatememory, nointegritychecks and testsigning. The policy is cut out of memory, and the legitimate hvloader.efi proceeds to load mcupdate_<vendor>.dll with nointegritychecks in effect - from ESP:\system32 rather than \Windows\system32, because the attacker's BCD sets device to the boot device and systemroot to \ [CONFIRMED].

That file is the attacker's, self-signed, and it runs with firmware's authority.

Three independent exploitation paths were published in the PoC repository in August 2022 [CONFIRMED]. ESET's assessment is that the BlackLotus developer adapted the PoC rather than discovering the bug: the timeline fits, and the implementation reuses the PoC's specific BCD tricks [HYPOTHESIS, ESET's].

4.5 Establishing persistence

The self-signed mcupdate*.dll runs an embedded MokInstaller UEFI application which [CONFIRMED]:

  1. restores the victim's original BCD from BCDR;
  2. copies the dropped Microsoft-signed shim over bootmgfw.efi;
  3. creates the MokList NVRAM variable containing the attacker's self-signed public certificate, formatted as an EFI_SIGNATURE_LIST exactly like db or dbx;
  4. deletes the exploit files from ESP:\system32 (the directory itself survives - a forensic artifact Microsoft explicitly calls out);
  5. reboots.

MokList is a Boot-Services-only variable. Writing it normally requires physical presence at the MOK Manager prompt. The exploit gave the attacker Boot Services with Secure Boot out of the way, so they wrote it programmatically. This is the single most important forensic artifact in the whole family: an unexpected certificate in MokList is durable, inspectable, and independent of the running OS.

Two details worth dwelling on:

The Defender kill is sloppy, and that is exploitable. Patching WdBoot.sys/WdFilter.sys entry points and stripping MsMpEng.exe token privileges both fail against a simple restart of the Defender process [CONFIRMED, ESET explicitly: "as this functionality is poorly implemented, it can be made ineffective by restarting the MsMpEng.exe process"]. A defender who knows this can keep AV alive.

The anti-removal is a denial-of-service tripwire. Protected handles cannot be closed by other processes; if someone succeeds anyway, the driver crashes the machine rather than allow the files to be deleted. Microsoft's IR team turned this into a detection: attempting to hash the protected ESP file with certutil returns ERROR_SHARING_VIOLATION on an infected machine [CONFIRMED, Microsoft]. A security control that blocks removal is also a fingerprint.

The IPC is MAC-derived and therefore predictable. Named section/event names come from the primary adapter's MAC with a small transform (octets below 16 have 16 added). For MAC 00-1c-0b-cd-ef-34: section \BaseNamedObjects\101c1b, event \BaseNamedObjects\Z01c1b [CONFIRMED]. Anyone who can create those objects can talk to the kernel driver - including sending its Uninstall BlackLotus command. ESET calls this the bootkit's weak point [CONFIRMED]. It is the closest thing to a clean removal path that exists, and it still leaves you on a machine you cannot trust.

4.7 Command and control

The HTTP downloader in winlogon.exe talks to /API/hpb_gate.php on a rotating set of domains, supports both user-mode and kernel payloads, and can create processes as SYSTEM (WTSQueryUserToken + CreateProcessAsUserW) [CONFIRMED]. Microsoft's IR team observed it also calling api.ipify.org over port 80 to learn the victim's public IP [CONFIRMED] - an excellent, low-false-positive hunt signal, because winlogon.exe has no business making outbound HTTP.

6.2 Which threat is hardest to detect, contain and eradicate?

Hardest to detect: MoonBounce. Not because of obfuscation - the hooks are crude - but because it removes every signal defenders use. There is no new file to enumerate, no deleted module, no disk artifact, no service, no registry key. A structural firmware inventory looks clean. The only reliable detection is a byte-level comparison against the OEM image for that exact model and firmware version, which requires an asset database most organisations do not maintain. It also runs before the OS, so every OS-resident detection control is downstream of it.

Hardest to contain: MoonBounce and LoJax equally, and for the same reason. Containment of a compromised machine is normally "isolate, image, rebuild". For these two, rebuild does not work: the implant re-establishes itself on the freshly installed OS at the next boot. Containment requires treating the hardware as the compromised asset - quarantine the physical machine, do not reissue it, do not put it back on the network after a wipe.

Hardest to eradicate: LoJax and MoonBounce. Eradication means rewriting the SPI flash from a trusted image using a hardware programmer, or replacing the motherboard, then proving the result by re-reading the flash and comparing it to a known-good image. There is no software procedure that is sufficient. ESET says this outright and it has not changed since 2018.

BlackLotus is the easiest of the three to eradicate and the most dangerous in practice, because it is cheap, commoditised, requires only admin rights, and defeats the control everyone believed was protecting them. It is also the one whose removal does not require physical hardware work - which is exactly why the firmware-resident ones remain the more serious long-term threat.

The uncomfortable conclusion: the ranking that matters for defenders is not sophistication but reachability. BlackLotus will be used against many targets because it is for sale; MoonBounce and LoJax will be used against few, but on those few, the machine is gone.

7. Detection and forensic strategy

7.1 Principle

Never let the compromised operating system be the sole witness. Each layer below is independent enough that an attacker has to defeat all of them, and the higher layers do not trust the lower ones.

Layer What it establishes Trusted because
0. Physical acquisition Ground truth about flash contents Out-of-band hardware, no OS involvement
1. Platform configuration Whether the flash could have been written Read from chipset registers/descriptor, not from OS claims
2. Firmware content integrity Whether flash contents match the vendor Cryptographic comparison to OEM release
3. Boot chain and NVRAM What firmware will load, and what it trusts Signed variable structure, read from firmware, not OS
4. Measured boot / TPM Whether the boot chain changed TPM PCRs, remote-verified
5. OS-visible artifacts Cheap, fast, high-recall hunting Corroboration only
6. Network Post-compromise activity Independent of the host

7.2 Layer 0-1: acquisition and platform posture

Acquire the flash out of band. In-circuit SPI read with a hardware programmer (e.g. CH341A-class or a professional programmer), or chip-off in a lab, with the host powered down and the SPI header isolated. Record: programmer model, firmware version, operator, timestamp, and the SHA-256 of the dump at acquisition. Acquisition of flash via the running OS is convenience, not evidence, because the thing you are measuring is the thing you are trusting to measure it.

Interrogate platform configuration. With the machine off-network and booted from a read-only live image, CHIPSEC is the practical tool (github.com/chipsec/chipsec):

  • BIOS write protections: BIOSWE, BLE, SMM_BWP in BIOS_CNTL;
  • protected ranges PR0-PR5 and FLOCKDN (flash descriptor lock);
  • descriptor region permissions (BIOS/ME/GbE read-write vs read-only);
  • SPI controller lock state and BIOS region access via the descriptor;
  • Boot Guard / verified boot status where the platform exposes it;
  • Secure Boot variable state (common.secureboot.variables).

Interpretation is blunt: if the BIOS region is writable from the OS, LoJax-class implants are one kernel bug away. That is a finding on its own, before any malware is found.

7.3 Layer 2: firmware content integrity (the one that catches MoonBounce)

The procedure:

  1. Obtain the OEM's signed firmware release for that exact model/version; unpack the vendor image, do not merely hash the capsule.
  2. Parse both images into volumes, files and sections (UEFITool, or uefi_firmware Python package, in the enclave, on copies).
  3. Compare per file GUID and per section, not per image. Record: file present in one but not the other; section content hash mismatch; section order change; free-space layout change; UI section names.
  4. For every PE/COFF module present in both, diff the code bytes. A module whose entry-point prologue differs from the vendor build is the MoonBounce signal.
  5. Keep a per-model reference manifest: GUID → SHA-256 of each section, plus the module's expected entry-point bytes. Once you have that, detection is a hash comparison, which runs at fleet scale.

Known-bad content scanning on top of the diff:

  • YARA over firmware volumes. Binarly's FwHunt rules (github.com/binarly-io/FwHunt) are built for exactly this, at the module level, and are the best starting corpus; Malpedia carries family rules for MoonBounce.
  • String hunts: SecDxe, c:\edk2\NtfsPkg\, autoche, rpcnetp, mcupdate_, When They Cry, hpb_gate, mboard.dll, VbsPolicyDisable. Treat hits as leads; several appear in clean firmware.

Do not skip the boring check: a firmware version that is older than the vendor's latest, on a machine that claims to be managed, is a finding. Downgrade is how implants survive updates.

7.4 Layer 3: boot chain and NVRAM

Read the variables, not the OS's opinion of them (efi-readvar from efitools, mokutil, or a firmware-level dump):

  • PK, KEK, db, dbx: unexpected certificates, unexpected emptiness, dbx older than the current Microsoft revocation list;
  • MokList / MokListRT: any certificate the organisation did not put there. This is the single highest-value BlackLotus indicator and it survives everything short of an NVRAM reset;
  • BootOrder, Boot####: unexpected entries, especially pointing at \EFI\Microsoft\Boot\grubx64.efi on a Windows-only fleet;
  • SecureBoot, SetupMode, VbsPolicyDisable, SaferMode / custom variables;
  • BCD: avoidlowmemory, truncatememory, nointegritychecks, testsigning, custom:22000023, and any device/systemroot pointing at the ESP rather than the OS partition [CONFIRMED, baton-drop mechanics].

Tooling on a live Windows host, for triage only: Confirm-SecureBootUEFI, Get-SecureBootUEFI, bcdedit /enum all, mountvol S: /S then inventory S:\EFI\Microsoft\Boot\, and compare those files against C:\Windows\Boot\EFI\ and against Microsoft's published hashes.

7.5 Layer 4: measured boot

Measured boot is the only mechanism that gives you a cryptographic statement about the boot chain, and it works against all three families:

  • Windows: C:\Windows\Logs\MeasuredBoot\*.log (TCG log, one per boot). Parse with tbs/TCGLogTools, diff between boots and between machines of the same build. BlackLotus appears as EV_EFI_Boot_Services_Application entries for grubx64.efi and an ESP-resident winload.efi [CONFIRMED, Microsoft].
  • Linux: tpm2_eventlog /sys/kernel/security/tpm0/binary_bios_measurements.
  • Remote verification: have the TPM quote PCRs to a verifier outside the machine's trust boundary. A machine that cannot produce a quote matching the golden value for its model/build is not trustworthy, regardless of what its OS reports.

Two honest caveats. (a) Measured boot tells you that something changed, not whether it is malicious - you need a golden baseline per model and firmware version. (b) If the firmware itself is the implant (LoJax, MoonBounce), the code doing the measuring may be the code being measured. That is why layer 0/2 (hardware read-back, vendor comparison) outrank layer 4 for firmware implants, and why DRTM late-launch (Intel TXT, AMD SKINIT) exists at all: it re-establishes a measurement root after the platform is already running.

Attribution is the weak point, not collection. On a host running any of these families, "which process owns this socket" is a claim by a compromised kernel: BlackLotus injects into winlogon.exe and MoonBounce into a SVCHOST.EXE -K NETSVCS instance precisely so that accounting names a signed Microsoft binary. qvault/netobs.py therefore records an attribution tier per flow (wire-only, claimed-only, corroborated, conflict) and reports the divergences between what the host says and what the wire saw. 7.8 Acquisition and preservation order

Do this in order, because each step can destroy the next:

  1. Network-isolate (switch port down, not just firewall rules) and photograph the machine's state.
  2. External evidence: TPM quote, Secure Boot variable dump, firmware version as reported by firmware setup. These are small and fast.
  3. Memory capture before shutdown, if memory-resident stages matter (MoonBounce, BlackLotus). Trusted-agent or hardware-assisted, never a tool installed onto the suspect OS at that moment.
  4. Disk image (write-blocked) including the ESP and any recovery partitions.
  5. SPI flash read-back out of band, powered down.
  6. Only then boot anything.

Everything captured goes into the vault described in §9, in that order, with the chain-of-custody record filled in as it happens.

7.9 What detection cannot do

It cannot tell you a machine is clean. It can tell you a machine matches a known baseline, or that a specific artifact is present. "No indicators found" on a firmware implant means "no indicators were found", which for MoonBounce may mean "we did not diff the right image".

8. Containment versus eradication, stated precisely

These are different objectives with different success criteria and it is worth being pedantic, because most incident reports blur them.

Containment Eradication
Goal Stop the threat acting and spreading Remove the malicious code and restore trust
For LoJax/MoonBounce Quarantine the physical machine; never reissue it; block its network identity; preserve evidence Rewrite SPI flash from a trusted image with a hardware programmer (or replace the board), then verify by read-back and comparison
For BlackLotus Isolate; block C2; preserve ESP, MOK and measured-boot evidence Reformat OS + ESP, revoke attacker MOK, apply dbx, remove unnecessary 3rd-party UEFI CA

Encryption belongs entirely on the containment side. It preserves; it never repairs. A vault full of encrypted LoJax samples is a vault full of encrypted LoJax samples, and the machine that produced them is still infected.

8.5 The six capability denials, and the plane that must enforce each

A containment plan is usually written as a list of things the subject may not do: reach the network, use authentication services, touch storage, consume compute, cross between networks, decrypt anything. Every one of those is enforceable at several layers, and the layers are not equivalent. The same six denials are either a real boundary or a sentence in a report, depending on which plane enforces them.

Plane Clean host OS compromised Firmware compromised
Subject OS (firewall, ACLs, AppLocker, service shutdown) full none none
Hypervisor (no vNIC, no vDisk) full full partial
Firmware platform (passwords, boot order, lock bits) partial none none
Network fabric (port ACL, VLAN isolation) full full full, if every egress path is enumerated
Physical (cable out, power off, media removed, write-blocker) full full full
Cryptographic (the key is simply absent) full full full

Two rows carry most of the argument:

Subject-OS enforcement disappears at exactly the moment you need it. Against a compromised kernel it is not a weakened control, it is a removed one: the attacker's code decides whether the rule applies. Against a DXE or SMM implant it never runs at all, because the implant executes before the OS exists. A plan built entirely from host firewalls, host ACLs and service disablement provides zero denial against any of the three families in this report once they are resident, while reading as complete on paper.

Decryption is a capability, not a location. No firewall, ACL, hypervisor or safe can deny it; they can only deny reach to the ciphertext, which is a different and weaker claim. Only the absence of key material denies decryption, which is why the vault's key management (§10) is the load-bearing control rather than any network rule.

qvault/containment.py encodes that table and can audit a claim: give it a plan, a posture and a list of capabilities you say you denied, and it reports which of them the plan does not actually support. It also refuses to claim eradication, always, because nothing in this table removes anything. LoJax survived OS reinstall and disk replacement, both far stronger than policy denial; a denial list that survives it is a change of what it can do, never of what is there.

9. Cryptographic quarantine: feasibility analysis

9.1 The proposal under evaluation

A suspicious firmware image or sample enters an isolated ingestion system. Before any analysis or execution-capable processing, the original object is cryptographically encapsulated. The encrypted object is stored in an isolated, immutable repository. Decryption is possible only through a separately controlled, explicitly authorised research environment.

This is a sound design for preservation, and the analysis below is about sharpening where it holds and where it silently fails. The reference implementation in qvault/ implements the parts that can be implemented in software honestly; §9.12 lists what must be hardware or process.

9.2 The ten questions, answered directly

1. Can encryption at ingestion prevent execution of the stored malware?

Yes, for the stored object; no, for the machine that produced it.

Once ciphertext is on disk, nothing can execute it: there is no loader that interprets AES-GCM output, no file association, no shell handler, no AV engine that will scan it (an AV engine cannot decrypt it, which is a feature), and no accidental double-click that does anything but produce an error. The stored object is inert only if three conditions hold:

  • encryption completes before any plaintext hits durable storage (no temp files, no volume-shadow copies, no page file on the ingestion host);
  • decryption keys are not present on the storage host;
  • nothing auto-decrypts on access (no FUSE mount, no auto-unlock keystore, no "preview" integration).

What encryption cannot do: it cannot stop the source machine's firmware implant from running at its next boot, and it cannot stop a human who is authorised to decrypt from then executing the sample. The last one is the dominant real-world risk and no cryptographic control touches it.

2. What happens if the ingestion host is already compromised?

Then everything downstream of the compromise is suspect, and the honest conclusion is that the object's provenance is unverified even though its ciphertext is intact. Concretely, a compromised ingestion host can:

  • read the plaintext in the 64 KiB window before encryption (by definition: it is the process doing the encryption);
  • compute and record whatever hashes it likes - the manifest is only as honest as the host that wrote it;
  • steal a DEK while it exists in RAM (during ingest) and, if it also obtains the wrapped DEK and KEK shares, decrypt later;
  • suppress or forge audit entries, unless the log is anchored somewhere the host cannot reach;
  • write a different object into the vault than the one it claims to have ingested (substitution), which no amount of post-hoc hashing can detect unless the expected hash came from an independent source.

Mitigations that actually work: make ingestion a disposable appliance that boots from read-only media, has no persistent writable storage except the vault target, and is re-imaged per case; derive the "expected" hash from an independent device (the flash programmer's own log, or a second reader); write the audit head to an external anchor the host cannot rewrite; treat objects ingested by an unverified host as provenance: unverified in the metadata, and never let that flag silently become "trusted".

3. Can firmware images be safely preserved without interpreting their contents?

Yes, and this is the correct default. A firmware image is a byte string; preservation means "these exact bytes, unmodified, with this custody record". Interpretation is a separate, later, optional activity that happens in the enclave on a copy.

This is also a security control. Firmware parsing entails LZMA/Tiano/EFI decompressors, PE loaders, FFS parsers and GUID databases - all of which have had memory-safety bugs. A vault that must parse a sample to store it has given an attacker a parser to attack at the exact moment its keys are in memory. The qvault.triage module therefore performs only bounded reads: magic numbers at fixed offsets, a flash-descriptor region table, string extraction over a capped window, and a Shannon-entropy histogram. It never decompresses, never maps, never loads.

4. How should encryption keys be managed?

Envelope encryption with split knowledge, in four layers:

Layer Key Where it lives Rotation
Object 256-bit DEK, unique per sample Only in RAM during ingest/retrieval; persisted only wrapped Never reused; destroyed after wrapping
Vault 256-bit KEK, wraps DEKs Split K-of-N (Shamir) across custodians; production: HSM/TPM-sealed shares Rewrap DEKs under a new KEK, no re-encryption of objects
Custodian Share-encryption key / HSM auth Smartcard, HSM slot, separate org unit Yearly, or on personnel change
Audit Ed25519 signing key Offline or HSM; public half anchored externally Yearly, with cross-signed transition

Rules that matter more than the algorithm choice: no key material on the storage host; no single person able to reconstruct the KEK; every key use logged separately from the vault audit log; tested restoration of keys from escrow; and a written, rehearsed procedure for what happens when a custodian leaves.

On "crypto-shredding": destroying a DEK renders one object permanently unreadable. That is a legitimate capability for data-protection compliance and a catastrophic one for evidence retention. In an evidence vault it should exist only as a dual-controlled, logged, last-resort action - never as an automatic retention policy.

5. Can individual samples have independent encryption keys?

Yes, and they must. qvault generates a fresh DEK per object. Consequences: compromise of one DEK exposes exactly one sample; a per-object key can be destroyed independently; a corrupted key affects one object rather than the archive; and there is no global key whose theft is a total loss. The cost is a wrapped-key blob per object (about 60 bytes), which is nothing.

6. How should researchers retrieve samples without exposing production?

Four rules, in order of importance:

  1. Decrypt to the enclave, never "download". The retrieval verb writes into a directory inside the analysis environment and nowhere else. qvault enforces this with a .enclave sentinel: a normal workstation directory is refused.
  2. Make retrieval a multi-party act. Threshold shares plus two independent approvals, with the requester unable to approve their own request.
  3. No bulk export. One object per authorised action, one audit entry per action. If an operator wants fifty samples, that is fifty decisions and fifty records.
  4. One-way results. Findings, logs and derived indicators leave the enclave; samples and memory images do not. Where a physical transfer is unavoidable, it is a logged, sealed, single-purpose medium destroyed afterwards.

7. How can the vault prevent automatic extraction and execution?

By removing every automated path that could touch a plaintext:

  • no key material on the storage host, so no daemon can decrypt;
  • no plaintext ever written by the vault outside a marked enclave (mode 0400, no execute bit - enforced and tested);
  • no auto-mount, no FUSE handler for .qv objects, no file association;
  • exclude the vault's object store from AV/EDR scanning, indexing, thumbnailing, cloud sync and backup agents that would otherwise need to read it (and would either copy plaintext or choke);
  • deny execve on the enclave output directory via mount options (noexec) and, where available, an LSM policy;
  • treat the enclave as hostile-after-use: revert to snapshot per case.

8. How should integrity and provenance be verified?

Layered, cheapest first:

  1. Ciphertext hash per object, recomputed on every verify pass (detects corruption and storage tampering).
  2. Plaintext hash recorded at ingest and re-checked after decryption (proves the bytes recovered are the bytes ingested).
  3. Metadata binding: the stream header and object identity are AEAD associated data, so ciphertext cannot be relabelled or swapped (tested).
  4. Hash-chained audit log: each entry commits to the previous one; edits and reordering break the chain (tested).
  5. Signed checkpoints: the log head is signed with Ed25519 so truncation is detectable (tested).
  6. External anchoring: a checkpoint hash published outside the vault's administrative domain - RFC 3161 timestamp, a transparency log, or a signed paper custody record. This is the only control that survives a compromised vault administrator, and it is a process control, not a code feature.
  7. Merkle root over the object set, published on the same cadence, so the inventory is committed to and not just individual objects (tested).

9. What happens if the vault's administrative environment is compromised?

Assume the attacker has root on the vault host. They can read all ciphertext (low value without the KEK), delete objects, rewrite envelopes and the audit log, fabricate approvals, alter policy, and - if the audit signing key is on that host - re-sign a rewritten history that verifies perfectly.

What still holds, if the design is right: objects they cannot read (no KEK); history they cannot rewrite undetectably (external anchors); and objects they cannot silently substitute (hashes derived from an independent source at acquisition). What fails: availability, and any object whose "expected" hash came from the compromised host.

Therefore the vault host must be treated as a semi-trusted component: it stores, it does not decide. Approvals should be signed by custodian-held keys, not by the server; the audit signing key belongs in an HSM or offline; the anchor belongs to a different administrative domain.

10. What attacks could bypass the quarantine boundary?

Enumerated with mitigations in §10. The short list: execution before encapsulation (previewers, indexers, sync clients, AV, autorun); key theft and out-of-band decryption; parser exploitation in triage; supply-chain compromise of the vault software itself; physical theft of shares; insider action; firmware or BMC implants in the ingestion appliance; DMA attacks against the enclave; plaintext re-introduced into general storage by a well-meaning analyst; and the simplest of all - an authorised person decrypting and running the sample.

9.3 Containment versus eradication (restated for the design)

The vault is a containment and preservation system with a provable property:

Between ingestion and authorised retrieval, the preserved object is not executable, not modified, and any modification of its stored form is detectable.

It is not an eradication system and must never be described as one. Specifically:

  • encrypting a LoJax sample does not clean the machine that produced it;
  • a clean vault does not imply a clean fleet;
  • a verified hash proves the sample is intact, not that the source was benign;
  • a verify: ok result means "the vault's own bytes are internally consistent".

10.6 Isolating the five components that interpret attacker bytes

The vault holds ciphertext, which is inert. Five components interpret bytes chosen by aggressors, and each is a code-execution surface whose input the enemy selects:

Component Must not have Why
Evidence reader network, keys, model, vault write, report emit parses firmware volumes and PE/FFS modules
Database parser the same hives and database files are a well-trodden parser exploitation surface
Indicator processing artifact read, finding write, whitelist write an enrichment pipeline with write access to detection state is a suppression primitive
Report generation artifact read, plaintext, keys, model a generator that reads artifacts can be injected by them; one that reads plaintext is an exfiltration route
AI-assisted analysis artifact read, plaintext, keys, finding write prompt-injectable by construction, and it may not author conclusions

Isolating these from the vault is the easy half. The hard half is isolating them from each other, because the dangerous paths are pairwise: a reader compromised by a crafted FFS volume that can reach the report generator can rewrite conclusions; indicator processing with write access can quietly withdraw a detection; and a model that can read decrypted artifacts is an exfiltration channel with a prompt-injection front door.

Three enforcement points in qvault/isolation.py:

  1. Placement is validated, not labelled. place() refuses a component in a zone that lacks what it needs or confers what it must not have, and reports both failures rather than the first.
  2. Data flow is deny-by-default. Plaintext artifacts may not leave the enclave toward the AI or presentation zones; key material flows only into the enclave; the model returns observation objects and receives redacted extracts.
  3. Model output is graded HYPOTHESIS with an unverified-output telemetry gap, so rule F3 lowers it to UNKNOWN until a human grades it. It enters as an Observation, never as a Finding, which means it cannot carry an attribution and cannot reach malicious_indicated on its own.

10.7 Firmware integrity is a separate subsystem, not a scanner check

An operating-system scan runs on the machine it judges, in a kernel the implant may be inside, through drivers the firmware may be deceiving. It cannot establish that UEFI firmware, the boot chain or device firmware is trustworthy. This is not a confidence problem that more coverage fixes; it is a direction problem, and the evidence route decides the question before the scan starts.

So qvault/firmware_integrity.py is a separate subsystem with its own vocabulary, and one rule: a verdict may not be stronger than the anchor that produced it.

Anchor Rank Establishes Grade
OS-mediated read 0 nothing —
Firmware measures itself 1 boot chain, DXE modules HYPOTHESIS
TPM quote 2 boot chain, DXE modules, option ROM CORROBORATED
Hardware boot ROM 3 CRTM, boot chain CORROBORATED
Out-of-band flash read 4 flash image, CRTM, DXE, ME/EC, option ROM CORROBORATED (CONFIRMED on two reads)

Three consequences worth stating plainly.

Host trust requires flash_image and crtm at rank 3 or above. There is exactly one admissible route to flash_image: an out-of-band read. certify_host() refuses on any weaker basis, naming the missing subject.

A clean TPM quote is not host trust. Measurement begins at the CRTM, so a compromised CRTM measures itself and the quote still verifies. PCRs detect post-CRTM tampering, which is valuable and is a different claim from "the firmware is intact".

os_scan_verdict() returns UNATTESTED whatever it found, including when every module matched. It exists so the refusal happens at the call site rather than in a design document nobody reads at 3am.

10.8 The detection engine may not certify its own integrity

This generalizes §10.7 from firmware to the whole pipeline. An engine that reports "I am intact" has produced a claim about itself from inside itself, and an implant that patches the detector's classifier patches the detector's self-check in the same write. Evidence direction decides the question before the check runs.

Four roles, one per component:

Role May Must not
Acquisition read artifacts, emit provenance, seal originals grade, attest its own coverage
Analysis parse, emit observations and findings, correlate write originals, clear its own audit entry, attest itself
Evidence storage hold originals and envelopes, append audit grade findings, rewrite history, attest itself
Trust verification measure, check manifests, verify the audit chain parse artifacts, grade, modify originals, attest itself

verify() refuses an attestation when the attestor is the subject, when both hold the same role (two analysis components are still one role), or when the "evidence" is the subject's own report relayed by an intermediary. Only the verification role may attest at all. Grade is capped by evidence class, and find_circular_trust() catches the case that looks rigorous and is not: two components reporting on each other, each presented as the other's corroboration.

The regress is stated rather than solved. A verifier must itself be anchored, so each attestation records its root; with no root the result is unverified with an unverified_root gap, because a verifier anchored in nothing outside the system is making exactly the claim this module refuses.

10.9 Scanning the scanner, and why "no false positives" is not a goal

An untrusted tool cannot be trusted to report that it is untrusted, and a detector cannot discover a trojan that edited the detector. Both statements are the same statement about evidence direction, and both are answered outside the code: a digest published with the release, a signed build, a hardware anchor, or read-only media.

integrity.py therefore refuses one specific thing. It will not accept a digest stored inside the tree it describes as proof of that tree, because an attacker who edits the module edits the recorded hash in the same change. A self-computed digest with no external reference is reported as unverified, never as verified, and the self-scan covers impersonation signals rather than only file hashes: interpreter startup hooks inside the package, import shadowing, world-writable code, .pth execution hooks, and bytecode with no source. Each carries its ordinary explanation, since every one of them is also something a legitimate packaging step does.

The device result is then read through the scanner's own status. A finding from an unattested instrument stands but is capped at INFERRED; an absence of findings from an unattested instrument is INCONCLUSIVE, because silence from a tool nobody has verified is not evidence about the device.

On error rates: "no false positives, no false negatives" is not a property a detector can have, and a tool printing it describes its author rather than its accuracy. calibration.py measures instead, reporting a confusion matrix over a labelled corpus with Wilson intervals, and it counts abstentions against accuracy rather than excluding them, since answering "inconclusive" to everything would otherwise score perfectly. Zero errors in n cases is reported as a bound (the rule of three: about 3/n at 95 percent), and refuse_zero_claim() rejects the sentence outright.

Two false positives in this module were found and fixed by its own tests: a normal __pycache__ was flagged as source-less bytecode because the source was sought in the wrong directory, and an installation absent from sys.path was treated as shadowed rather than as an answerable unknown.

11.1 Residual-risk statement

After all mitigations, the following remain true and should be written into any risk register that adopts this design:

  1. The trustworthiness of a sample's provenance is bounded by the trust you place in the acquisition appliance and its operator. Cryptography cannot recover from "the capture device lied".
  2. The enclave is the weakest link by design, because that is where plaintext and execution live. Its isolation is physical and procedural, not cryptographic.
  3. Firmware-resident threats are not remediated by any software control, including this one.
  4. Authorised collusion defeats dual control. It is a control against mistakes and single insiders, not against a conspiracy.
  5. Revocation lag is structural. CVE-2022-21894 was fixed in January 2022 and the vulnerable binaries were still not revoked when BlackLotus was found in the wild in 2023, which is why the bootkit worked on fully patched systems.

11.2 The four architectural limitations, and what each control cannot buy

Reading report/ARCHITECTURAL_LIMITATIONS.md in full is worthwhile; the summary is that the four controls proposed for these systems increase coverage and none of them increases integrity.

Limitation Consequence Control What the control cannot buy
Limited live-state visibility historical records miss an active compromise optional live response integrity: live state is read by the kernel the implant may be inside
Firmware blind spots OS-level scans cannot establish firmware integrity independent firmware acquisition coverage without physical access, or proof that in-circuit read-back equals the chip
Baseline limitations legitimate unusual behaviour looks suspicious per-device baselines validity when the baseline was learned after compromise, or resistance to slow drift
Indicator trust poisoned intelligence produces bad findings provenance and independent validation independence when feeds resell one upstream; and poisoning most cheaply causes false negatives

Two qualifiers are load-bearing and easy to lose. "Independent" firmware acquisition means the path does not pass through the subject: a flash read performed by a driver in a possibly compromised OS is a claim about the OS, not about the flash. And a baseline captured after compromise does not merely fail, it certifies the implant as normal, which is worse than having no baseline at all.

qvault/limitations.py encodes this as evidence planes with a forgeable_by_subject property, so a claim's strength is computed from the plane it rests on rather than asserted in prose.

Two implementation notes worth calling out, because both were found by testing rather than by design:

  • The first version of the GF(256) tables used 2 as the generator. It is not primitive under the AES polynomial (order 51), so Shamir reconstruction silently produced wrong keys. The tables now use 3, and a threshold test pins the property that K-1 shares yield garbage rather than an error - below the threshold there should be no signal and no oracle.
  • The first flash-descriptor parser reported a phantom region on synthetic input. Region entries are now validated against the image size, because a parser at the boundary that invents structure is worse than one that admits ignorance.

15. Unresolved research questions

Stated as questions, with what evidence would settle each. These are the gaps I would chase next, in priority order.

Fourteen of them cannot be answered by reading anything at all — they need bench hardware. Those are collected separately in report/HARDWARE_TEST_QUESTIONS.md, with rig, experiment, and what result would confirm or refute the current assumption. Three of them (firmware-update coverage, measured-boot sensitivity, OS-writability of the BIOS region) would change this report's conclusions if they came out the other way.

  1. How was MoonBounce's firmware written? Kaspersky states the infection vector is unknown. Settling it needs either the operator tooling recovered from a victim network, or telemetry of a flash write on an affected endpoint. Until then, every claim about "remote firmware infection" is a hypothesis.
  2. ~~Which specific loader/kernel functions does MoonBounce hook?~~ Resolved in §3.8: OslArchTransferToKernel in winload.efi, then ExAllocatePool in ntoskrnl, reached via a retfq-tail patch and a relocation-directory code cave. What remains open is how many loader builds the 0x158878-byte scan window covers, since that offset is build-specific and bounds which Windows versions the implant can infect.
  3. How many LoJax-era platforms actually had writable BIOS regions in 2018, and how many still do? ESET says the tool worked on misconfigured or pre-PCH hardware. A CHIPSEC survey across a fleet would convert that into a number, and that number is the real exposure metric.
  4. Does any vendor firmware update preserve a malicious DXE file? ESET says a full-region rewrite removes LoJax, but partial/region-preserving update mechanisms exist. Testing this per vendor is unglamorous and directly determines whether "update firmware" is remediation or theatre.
  5. Is MokList audited anywhere at fleet scale? It is the highest-value BlackLotus indicator and almost certainly not monitored in most estates. A measurement study would be useful.
  6. What is the actual revocation lag distribution for dbx entries? BlackLotus exploited a gap of more than a year. Quantifying the distribution across vendors would turn an anecdote into a risk parameter.
  7. Can an SMM-based implant be detected from inside the OS at all? Out of scope here (none of the three families use SMM), but it is the next layer down and the answer is probably "only with hardware assistance".
  8. What does Boot Guard actually attest in practice, per OEM? The specification defines verified and measured modes; how each OEM configures fuse policy is opaque, which makes trust decisions hard.
  9. Does any of the three survive a vendor firmware capsule update in the field? Requires controlled experiment on real hardware per model. This is the single most decision-relevant unknown for remediation guidance.
  10. How do you verify an enclave is clean, not merely isolated? DRTM gives a measurement, but the verifier and the golden values are themselves trust anchors. A worked design for a small lab would be a genuine contribution.

16. Closing assessment

The three families tell a coherent story about where the attack surface actually is. LoJax abused a legitimate persistence channel (anti-theft firmware) plus a signed hardware-access driver. MoonBounce abused the fact that nobody compares firmware bytes. BlackLotus abused a trust mechanism - shim and MOK - designed to make Secure Boot usable, and got permanent persistence out of a one-time vulnerability.

None of them required a novel firmware exploit. All three required defenders to have left something unmeasured: flash write protections, firmware content, or boot-chain trust state.

That is the argument for the vault. Not that encryption defeats these threats - it does not, and any design that says so is wrong - but that preservation without execution is a precondition for studying them at all, and that a custody chain which can prove "these exact bytes, unmodified, never executed here" is the foundation every later conclusion rests on.

The vault preserves. People eradicate. The architecture should never let anyone confuse the two.
